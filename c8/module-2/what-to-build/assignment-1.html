Assignment 1 · Level 1 · Both tracks

First Proof

North Star first. Then one incident from your own life, one hypothesis that can fail, and one link a stranger can use.

Follows: Idea to AppSelf-paced, about 21 days

The brief

Establish your North Star and get it to the readiness check. Then bring one relevant incident from your own lived experience, run discovery to a falsifiable hypothesis and a manual MVP plan, publish a tracker for that plan, deliver the value by hand to 2 to 5 real people, and write down the input, process and output you used.

01Why this exists

  • Problem first. Most products fail because someone built a real solution to a problem nobody had. Investigate the problem, attack your own assumptions, and run a cheap experiment before you build anything.
  • Destination before problem. A real problem is not automatically the right one for you. Your North Star decides which experiences are worth examining, because it says what you are trying to prove. Without it, every project looks equally relevant.
  • Manual on purpose. The output is not an app. It is a process you have run once by hand, with a number attached, and a hypothesis you now know to be truer or falser than it was.

02Step one: your North Star

A good North Star is a personally chosen, time-bound career or business outcome, made concrete by the responsibility or value you want to create and the observable evidence that would make that outcome credible.

It answers: by when, what do I want to be doing or achieving, for whom, at what level or scale, and what would demonstrate that I am able to deliver it? It does not need to predict your whole future. It needs to be clear enough to guide the next meaningful choice.

  • First. Establish your North Star before choosing an observation, a problem or a project. When the destination is unclear, fix it first.
  • Proof, not just a reward. A role and salary describe a destination; they do not explain readiness. Name the proof: who would benefit, what would change, what you would own, and how someone could check it.
  • Value, not output. Shipping projects or attracting users shows output, not value. Say what would become measurably better for the people involved.
  • Horizon. Separate the long-term ambition, the outcome for your current working horizon, and the next milestone. A long goal can stay; add a nearer milestone so progress can be judged now.
  • Meaning. Faster, better, efficient and go-to need an observable meaning: what is counted, for whom, over what period, against which comparison.
  • Honest numbers. Unknown baselines stay unknown until measured. Do not invent a precise percentage to look rigorous.
  • What you control. Keep outcomes others or the market control as the aspiration; measure the contribution you can demonstrate directly.
  • One direction. Name one primary destination for this horizon. Record the others separately.
  • Yours. Write it in your own words. AI can sharpen the wording; it cannot supply your motivation, experience or commitment.

Write it as a short statement with a supporting note. Not every part belongs in one sentence.

PartWhat to write
StatementBy [date], I intend to [career or business outcome] in/for [context], at [relevant level, income or scale]. I will support this with evidence that I can [responsibility or capability], producing [observable value] for [people], checked through [evidence or verifier].
Supporting noteWhy I want this. What I currently do or know. What I expect the proof to demonstrate. What remains an assumption or needs measurement. What I am choosing not to pursue during this horizon.

The readiness check. Before the observation, a reviewer must be able to state your chosen outcome and responsibility, the relevant context, your working horizon, and the intended form of proof. Each must be at least partly specified, and the open questions must not change which experiences are relevant. Measurement details can sharpen later, once you have a real incident. Completed proof is not an entry requirement: a North Star is a future outcome.

Write and review yours in the North Star workbench. Each review names what is already clear and asks one question about the gap that matters most. Read how to write a good North Star first, with before-and-after examples from real reviews.

Once it passes the check, stop polishing the wording. Bring one real incident.

03The five steps after it

  1. Bring one incident. Look back over the last seven days of your work or life for one incident relevant to your North Star: who tried to do what, where the difficulty occurred, and what happened next. One real person, one real situation, at the depth of what is on their desk at 12:38 AM, what they do, what breaks, how long it takes. Your lived experience, not a borrowed story, with no solution in it. It is relevant to your North Star: it is a chance to build the proof your North Star names.
  2. Run discovery to a hypothesis. In the app, Stations 1 to 3 walk you through it one question at a time. Outside the app, install the discovery-to-mvp-coach skill file (Settings, Customise, Skills, upload) and invoke it with /discovery-to-mvp-coach. Budget 60 to 90 minutes. Answer every question yourself and add assumptions it missed. Do the simulated interviews properly and keep the 4 to 5 questions it grades you on. Save the discovery document and the MVP plan.
  3. Publish the tracker. Turn the MVP plan into a tracker of every line item in the 21-day pilot (the app builds one from Station 4; or use Lovable or any AI builder). The link must open for a stranger without your help.
  4. Run the manual MVP. Deliver the value by hand to 2 to 5 real people in the first 14 days, asking Mom Test questions: their life, their past, specifics, emotions. Update the tracker daily.
  5. Write the process. Fill the card in section 04 for what you actually did.

04Input, process, output

Every piece of software reduces to these three words.

FieldWhat to writeStand-up example
InputThe raw material, its format, where it comes from, how often it arrives. Attach one real sample.Messy transcript of the weekly Zoom stand-up, one file per call
OutputWhat the recipient gets, its format, who receives it, and the decision it lets them make.One-page summary of last week's calls, emailed to the founder, brief enough to act on

Do not automate anything on this card during the 21 days.

05Rules of the build

  • North Star first. No observation, problem or project advice until your North Star passes the readiness check.
  • Manual first. No agents, no automation, no product. Use an LLM by hand if it helps you do a step, and write down the prompt as part of the process.
  • Your observation, not a borrowed one. "People want to automate workflows so I will build five agents" is rejected on sight.
  • The hypothesis must be able to fail. Subject, problem, cause, intervention, a number, a deadline, and the result that would prove you wrong.
  • Real people, not only the simulation. Phase 6 in the skill is practice. It does not count toward the user research conversations.
  • Label what you know. Target, assumption, reported, verified within scope, illustrative, unknown. An assumption is allowed; an assumption disguised as a result is not.
  • One cycle at a time. Run one observation to the end of validation before starting the next. If it dies, that is a result: log it and run the next one.
  • LLM assistance. Do not generate the observation with an LLM: it is the one input that is in no training set, and the only part that is yours.

DeliverableWhat it must show

07After submission

What comes next begins with your Input-Process-Output card, not with a hypothetical. The process on your card becomes a system of deterministic and probabilistic parts. None of that lands without a process you have already run by hand, aimed by a North Star that says what it should prove.

Cohort zero was 10 people, Rs 21,000 and 21 days with no software, testing one hypothesis with a 60% bar. Nine of ten shipped a credible demo. That was the first proof.
